Assumptions & Methodology

A full description of every input, formula, and rate used to generate projections. All Canadian government benefit figures reflect published 2025–2026 values.

📊 Projection Framework
How the model works
Model type
Deterministic year-by-year simulation. Each year from your current age to the plan-to age, the model applies growth, contributions (pre-retirement), then withdrawals (post-retirement) in sequence.
Inflation adjustment
All spending targets and government benefits are grown forward using the inflation rate each year: value × (1 + inflation)^years. KPI summaries report government benefits in today's purchasing power (nominal ÷ inflation factor).
Real vs. nominal portfolio
The "Real vs. Nominal" chart deflates each year's portfolio total by (1 + inflation)^years to show what your balance is worth in today's dollars — useful for understanding true purchasing power over time.
Sequence of operations
each year
Accumulation years: Apply investment return → Add annual contributions.
Retirement years: Apply investment return → Calculate government income → Withdraw from accounts in order (Non-Reg → TFSA → RRSP/RRIF) to cover spending gap → Apply RRIF minimums if applicable.
📈 Investment Return
Portfolio growth rate
Default return rate default 5.5%
Applied uniformly to all three account types (RRSP/LIRA, TFSA, Non-Registered) each year before contributions or withdrawals. This represents a blended nominal annual return on a balanced portfolio (roughly 60% equities / 40% fixed income).
Compounding method
Annual compounding. Each account balance is multiplied by (1 + return rate) at the start of each year, before contributions are added or withdrawals are taken.
Limitations
The model uses a fixed rate — it does not simulate market volatility, sequence-of-returns risk, or Monte Carlo distributions. Real portfolios will vary year to year. Consider modelling a conservative rate (e.g. 4–5%) alongside an optimistic one (e.g. 7%) to bracket outcomes.
🍁 Government Benefits
CPP and OAS rates & adjustments
CPP 2026 maximum Canada
The 2026 maximum CPP retirement pension at age 65 is $1,507.65/month. The default input is $900/month, representing an average benefit for someone with a mixed employment history. Adjust to your personal CPP Statement of Contributions estimate.
CPP early/late adjustment formula
Taking CPP before 65 reduces the benefit by 0.6% per month (7.2%/year). Taking it after 65 increases it by 0.7% per month (8.4%/year), up to a maximum of +42% at age 70.
multiplier = 1 − ((65 − startAge) × 12 × 0.006) [if before 65] multiplier = 1 + ((startAge − 65) × 12 × 0.007) [if after 65]

OAS is indexed quarterly to the Consumer Price Index. The model applies the 10% automatic supplement at age 75 within the simulation loop.
OAS deferral adjustment
Deferring OAS past 65 increases the benefit by 0.6% per month, up to a maximum of +36% at age 70. The model applies this multiplier to the base monthly amount entered.
Inflation indexing of benefits
In the simulation, CPP and OAS are grown forward annually by the inflation rate entered. This approximates the real-world CPI indexing these programs receive, keeping benefits in line with purchasing power over time.
OAS clawback (GIS/OAS recovery)
The model does not simulate the OAS recovery tax (clawback), which begins at ~$93,454 of net income in 2025. If your projected retirement income is high, actual OAS may be partially or fully clawed back. Consult a financial advisor if this applies to you.
💸 Tax Treatment
How taxes are applied to each account type
Tax rate input default 22%
A single flat average marginal tax rate applied to all taxable income. This is a simplification — actual Canadian tax is graduated across federal and provincial brackets. Use your estimated effective rate on income above the basic personal amount.
RRSP / RRIF withdrawals taxable
Withdrawals are grossed up to cover the tax:
gross withdrawal = net amount needed ÷ (1 − tax rate) net received = gross withdrawal × (1 − tax rate)
This means if you need $10,000 after-tax and your rate is 22%, the model withdraws $12,821 from your RRSP.
TFSA withdrawals tax-free
TFSA withdrawals are completely tax-free. The model withdraws exactly the gap amount needed — no gross-up is applied. This is a key advantage of TFSA over RRSP in retirement drawdown.
Non-registered withdrawals
Treated as fully taxable income in the model (no capital gains / dividend preferential treatment). In practice, capital gains are only 50% included in income (or 66.67% above the $250,000 annual threshold as of 2024 proposals). The model is conservative for non-reg accounts.
Government benefits taxation
CPP and OAS are taxable income. The model reduces the gross government benefit by the tax rate:
net government income = (CPP + OAS annual) × (1 − tax rate)
🔁 Withdrawal Strategy
Order accounts are drawn down in retirement
Drawdown sequence
When government income (CPP + OAS) doesn't cover the spending target, the model draws from accounts in this order:

1. Non-Registered — drawn first to reduce taxable growth and defer tax-sheltered accounts
2. TFSA — drawn second; tax-free withdrawal preserves RRSP balance longer
3. RRSP / RRIF — drawn last; taxable, so deferred as long as possible
Limitations
This is a simplified, fixed-order strategy. Optimal real-world drawdown often involves drawing from all three accounts simultaneously for tax bracket management. A financial planner can model personalized melt-down strategies.
📋 RRIF Minimum Withdrawals
Mandatory RRIF drawdown rules
Conversion age Canada
RRSPs must be converted to a RRIF by December 31 of the year you turn 71. The model begins enforcing RRIF minimums at age 72 (the first year a minimum withdrawal is required after conversion).
Minimum withdrawal rates

Excess RRIF income handling
If the RRIF minimum exceeds what is needed to cover the spending gap, the excess (net of tax) flows into the Non-Registered account. This reflects a common real-world strategy of parking forced RRIF withdrawals in non-reg.
🛍 Spending Model
How retirement spending evolves over time
Flat (Inflation) model
Spending grows each year solely by the inflation rate. Your real (inflation-adjusted) spending stays constant throughout retirement — a simple, conservative assumption.
Smile Curve model default
Based on research by David Blanchett (Morningstar, 2013) and others showing retirees typically spend more in early active retirement, less in mid-retirement, then more again in late retirement (health care). The model applies:
Ages 65–74: multiplier = 1.00 (full spending, "Go-Go" years) Ages 75–84: multiplier decreases by 2% per year, flooring at 0.80 Ages 85+: multiplier increases by 3.5% per year, capping at 1.15
All spending is also indexed forward by inflation each year.
💹 Withdrawal Rate & 4% Rule
How the withdrawal rate KPI is calculated
Calculation formula
The withdrawal rate shown in the KPI card is calculated at the first year of retirement:
withdrawal rate = (total portfolio withdrawals in year 1) ÷ (total portfolio at retirement) × 100
Portfolio withdrawals include Non-Reg + TFSA + gross RRSP withdrawal.
4% guideline
The 4% rule (Bengen, 1994) is a widely cited guideline suggesting a 4% initial withdrawal rate from a balanced portfolio is sustainable over a 30-year retirement. The model flags rates above 4% as a caution. This is a heuristic, not a guarantee — especially relevant given Canadian tax structures and CPP/OAS offsets.
Government income offset
CPP and OAS income directly reduces how much you need to draw from your portfolio. A strong CPP/OAS combination can significantly lower your withdrawal rate, making the 4% guideline more achievable even with a modest nest egg.
